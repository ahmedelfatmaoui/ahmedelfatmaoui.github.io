How much insurance?

Insurance is not all or nothing. A person chooses how much coverage to buy, weighing what one more dollar of coverage adds when ill against what its premium costs when well.

The benefit and cost of coverage

A person has $100,000 and a 0.10 chance of an illness that costs $50,000. They can buy any amount of coverage, q, at a premium of 20 cents per dollar of coverage: $1,000 for a $5,000 policy.

Coverage helps only when they are ill: each dollar of coverage pays $1 but costs 20 cents, a net gain of 80 cents. When they stay well, the premium is a pure cost.

$0$50,000 (the whole loss)

The utility worksheet adds up the graph: the area under MB is the expected utility coverage brings when ill, the area under MC is what its premiums cost when well, and the green area between them is the net gain.

Weighing the next dollar of coverage

Marginal benefit (MB, blue): the expected utility the next dollar of coverage adds when ill. Marginal cost (MC, red): the expected utility its premium takes away when well. Both are measured in utility, so the graph’s vertical scale is an index.

Key terms

Coverage
The amount the policy pays if the loss happens.
Premium rate
The price per dollar of coverage: a 20% premium means $0.20 for each $1 of coverage.
Marginal benefit of coverage
The expected utility gained from one more dollar of coverage, which pays off only when ill. It falls as coverage rises, because each extra dollar raises wealth when ill, where marginal utility is already lower.
Marginal cost of coverage
The expected utility given up by paying that dollar’s premium, felt when well. It rises with coverage, because each premium dollar comes out of wealth that is already lower.

Try this: slide coverage from $5,000 to $10,000. Which way do A and A′ move, and why?

The best amount of coverage

At $5,000 of coverage, the marginal benefit (A) is far above the marginal cost (A′): another dollar of coverage adds more than it costs, so the person buys more. At $10,000 (B and B′) the gap is smaller.

Buy until marginal benefit equals marginal cost

The gap closes at point X, where MB = MC. That coverage, q*, is the best amount: about $30,000, less than the $50,000 loss. Past X, another dollar costs more than it adds.

$0$50,000

Watch the expected column as you move the coverage line. The net gain (green area) grows while each extra $1,000 adds more than it costs, peaks at X, and shrinks once the red area appears: buying past X gives back utility.

Key terms

Optimal coverage, q*
The amount of coverage at which the marginal benefit of the last dollar equals its marginal cost.
Partial insurance
Coverage below the full loss. A person chooses it when the premium rate is above the chance of loss, as here (0.20 versus 0.10).

Try this: drag the coverage line to the right of X. Which curve is now higher, and what does that tell the person to do?

A higher premium

Raise the premium from 20 to 25 cents per dollar of coverage: $1,250 for the $5,000 policy. Each dollar of coverage now nets only 75 cents when ill, so MB shifts down to MB2. Each dollar also costs more when well, so MC shifts up to MC2.

10%35%

The new optimum Y is at q**, about $23,000. People react to higher premiums by buying less coverage: the demand for insurance slopes down.

Key terms

Actuarially fair premium rate
A premium per dollar of coverage equal to the chance of loss (here 10%). At this rate a risk-averse person buys full coverage.
Loading
The part of the premium above the fair rate, which pays the insurer’s costs. The bigger the loading, the less coverage people buy.

Try this: choose the fair 10% premium. How much coverage does the person buy now?

A bigger expected loss

Back at a 20% premium, suppose illness would cost $75,000 instead of $50,000. Wealth when well does not change, so MC stays put. But wealth when ill is now much lower ($29,000 with a $5,000 policy), where each extra dollar matters more: MB shifts up to MB2.

$50,000$80,000

The new optimum Z, at q***, is higher, about $35,000: a bigger expected loss raises the demand for coverage.

Key terms

Expected loss
The chance of loss times its size: 0.10 × $75,000 = $7,500 here, up from $5,000.

Try this: raise the loss step by step from $50,000. Which curve moves, which stays put, and why?

More wealth

Now suppose the person starts with $125,000 instead of $100,000, with the same $50,000 loss and 20% premium. Two things change at once:

  • The coverage pays off at a higher wealth, where it adds less utility: MB shifts down.
  • The premium comes out of a higher wealth, where it costs less utility: MC shifts down too.

The new optimum W can be above or below q*: it depends on which shift is larger. Compare three people:

A stylized model for teaching. As in Figures 13.2 and 13.3, marginal benefit and marginal cost are straight lines on an index scale, and each change shifts them parallel to the originals: a higher premium moves MB down and MC up, a bigger loss moves MB up, and more wealth moves both down. The shifts are sized so the best coverage is $30,000 at a 20% premium, about $23,000 at 25%, the full loss at the fair 10% premium, and about $35,000 for a $75,000 loss. The worksheet amounts are exact.
