Why people buy insurance (risk aversion)

Insurance usually costs more than the losses it is expected to cover, yet people buy it. The reason is the shape of one curve: when each extra dollar adds less satisfaction than the one before, a sure amount of wealth beats a gamble with the same average.

Each extra dollar adds less

The curve shows the total utility (satisfaction) a person gets from each level of wealth they are sure to have. Wealth means everything they own, in dollars. At $50,000 their utility is 140. At $100,000 it is 200.

Doubling wealth does not double utility. The first $50,000 buys necessities and matters a lot; the second $50,000 adds only 60 more.

Diminishing marginal utility of wealth

The amber line is the slope of the curve: the extra utility from the next $1,000, called the marginal utility of wealth. It gets flatter as wealth rises, so the curve is bowed (concave). This shape is what makes people dislike risk.

Key terms

Cardinal utility
Utility measured in units, so the size of gains can be compared, not just ranked (ordinal utility). Insurance analysis needs it to tell that marginal utility is falling.
Marginal utility of wealth
The extra utility from one more dollar (here, the next $1,000): the slope of the curve.
Diminishing marginal utility
Each extra dollar adds less than the one before, so the curve is bowed (concave).

Try this: move wealth from $50,000 to $60,000, then from $90,000 to $100,000. Both add $10,000. Which adds more utility? You can also drag the point along the curve.

Facing a risk

Now the person has $100,000 but may fall ill. Illness brings $50,000 of medical bills, leaving $50,000. Without insurance, the year ends at B (well) or at A (ill).

Expected wealth = (1 − p) × $100,000 + p × $50,000
= $95,000

Expected utility = (1 − p) × 200 + p × 140
= 194

Here p is the chance of illness. Expected utility lies on the red line from A to B, at point C above expected wealth. The likelier illness is, the closer C slides to A.

An average, not an outcome

No one actually ends the year at C. Each person lands at A or at B; C is the average over many such years or people.

Key terms

Expected value
The probability-weighted average of the outcomes: E = p1R1 + p2R2 + … + pnRn. For wealth, this is expected wealth.
Expected utility
The probability-weighted average of the utilities of the outcomes, read off the straight line from A to B (point C).

Try this: set the chance of illness to 0.5. Where does C land, and what is expected utility?

A sure thing beats the gamble

An insurer offers full coverage: pay a premium, and if illness strikes it pays the $50,000 of bills. Suppose the premium equals the expected payout, p × $50,000. This is an actuarially fair premium: the insurer’s expected payouts just equal the premiums it takes in.

Well: $100,000 − premium. Ill: $50,000 + $50,000 − premium.
Either way: $95,000, for sure.

Same average wealth, more utility

Point D is on the curve directly above C. The person has the same expected wealth either way, but a sure amount gives more utility than a gamble with that average. The green bar running straight up from C to D is the utility gained by removing the risk; its size is printed beside the utility scale.

Key terms

Actuarially fair premium
A premium equal to the expected payout (chance of loss × size of loss), so expected benefits paid out equal premiums taken in.

The most they would pay

Real insurers also cover administration and other costs, so premiums run above the fair level. How high can a premium go before the person would rather stay uninsured?

Follow the amber line left from C to the curve, at point F. A sure $80,000 gives the same utility, 194, as the gamble. So the person would pay up to $100,000 − $80,000 = $20,000 for full coverage.

The segment FC measures risk aversion

Of that $20,000, $5,000 is the expected loss (the fair premium). The other $15,000, the length of the highlighted segment FC, is the risk premium: what they would pay just to be rid of the risk. The more bowed the curve, the longer FC.

$5,000 (fair)$50,000 (the whole loss)

Why this range? Below the fair $5,000 the insurer would expect to pay out more than it collects, so no insurer offers it; that would put I to the right of D. Above $50,000 the premium would exceed the loss itself. At $50,000, I reaches A: paying the whole loss for sure leaves the person as badly off as being ill.

The bracket along the bottom marks the premium, and point I shows where it leaves them, insured. The bar at I measures the change in utility against the gamble’s level (194):

  • Green, above: insuring gains utility, so they buy.
  • Red, below: insuring loses utility, so they stay uninsured.
  • At $20,000 I lands on F: no gain or loss, so they are indifferent.
  • At $5,000 (the fair premium) I is at D, the largest gain.

The difference between the most they would pay and the premium is their consumer surplus.

Key terms

Loading costs
Administration and other costs insurers add to the fair premium to break even, pushing real premiums above it.
Certainty equivalent
The sure wealth that gives the same utility as the gamble: point F, $80,000.
Risk premium
What the person would pay above the fair premium just to be rid of the risk: the distance FC ($15,000), a measure of risk aversion.
Self-insurance
Bearing the risk yourself (staying uninsured), as the person does when the premium is above their maximum.

What the graph shows

  1. People pay more than the fair premium because marginal utility of wealth diminishes: they are risk-averse, so removing risk is worth something to them.
  2. A premium above the fair level leaves them with less expected wealth than staying uninsured. They still buy, because removing the risk raises their utility.
  3. The higher the chance of illness, the more they would pay in total, because the expected loss grows.

Watch the amber part. The risk premium does not keep rising with the chance of illness. It peaks near p = 0.3 here and shrinks toward zero as illness becomes almost certain: a near-certain loss is an expected cost, not much of a risk.

Key terms

Risk-averse
Prefers a sure amount to a gamble with the same expected value; marginal utility of wealth diminishes. Will pay more than the fair premium.

Check yourself

This lesson covers the standard explanation: risk-averse people buy insurance to remove risk. Chapter 13 also presents another view: Nyman (1999) argues that insurance also transfers income from the healthy to the sick, paying for care the sick could not otherwise afford.
