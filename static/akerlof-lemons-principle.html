The lemons principle

When one side of a market knows more than the other, the best units leave at every price. Sellers who know more drive good cars out of the used-car market; buyers who know more drive low risks out of health insurance.

Who knows what?

Nine used cars are for sale, each equally likely. Their quality Q runs from 0, a lemon, to 2, a plum, in steps of ¼. Each owner knows their own car’s quality. Buyers know only that every quality is equally likely.

Owner’s value = $5,000 × Q Buyer’s value = $7,500 × Q

Every buyer values every car (with Q above 0) more than its owner does. If buyers could see quality, every one of these cars would sell.

0 (lemon)2 (plum)

Key terms

Asymmetric information
One side of a market knows more than the other. Here the seller knows the car’s quality; the buyer does not.
Cardinal quality index
Q measures how much quality, not just rank: a car with Q = 2 has twice the quality of one with Q = 1.

The auctioneer lowers the price

An auctioneer calls out a price, P. An owner offers a car only if it is worth less to them than the price: 5,000 Q ≤ P. Better cars stay home. Buyers cannot tell the offered cars apart, so they will pay for the average car offered.

$0$10,000

    Key terms

    Lemons principle
    Under asymmetric information, the bad drives out the good: at every price the best units are withdrawn, which lowers what buyers will pay, which drives out the next best.

    Try this: press “Next round” several times. Which cars leave each time, and does the market ever settle?

    Why no price works

    Each round, the auctioneer tries what buyers offered last time. Follow the rounds down the table. Select a row to see that round on the graph.

    At price P, only cars with Q ≤ P/5,000 are offered. Quality is spread evenly from 0, so the average car offered is about half the cutoff:

    average Q ≈ P / 10,000 ⇒ buyers pay at most 7,500 × P / 10,000 ≈ 0.75 P

    The bad drives out the good

    Buyers always offer about three quarters of the asking price, so no price satisfies both sides, even though buyers value every car (with Q above 0) more than its owner does. The market unravels.

    Key terms

    Market unraveling
    Mutually beneficial trades fail to happen because the information gap pushes good units out of the market one round after another.

    Try this: compare the last two columns in each row. Is there any round where buyers offer as much as the asking price?

    Health insurance: the mirror image

    People with the same demographics expect different health spending, spread evenly from $0 to $M. The insurer must break even, so the premium must cover the pool’s average spending. Now the buyer knows more: each person knows their own expected spending, while the insurer knows only the spread.

    Anyone who expects to spend less than the premium won’t pay it. They leave, the pool gets costlier, and the premium must rise.

      Key terms

      Adverse selection
      When buyers know their own risk better than the insurer, high risks are the most eager to buy, so the insured pool is riskier than average.
      Death spiral
      Low risks leave, the premium rises to cover the costlier pool, more people leave, and so on, until the market may disappear.

      Simplifications: people know their spending only roughly, and risk aversion makes some low risks willing to pay a bit more than their expected loss (Chapter 13). The tendency is still real, and it shapes how insurance markets are regulated.
